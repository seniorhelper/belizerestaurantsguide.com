Drinks

What to drink in Belize: Belikin, rum, juices and hot sauce

The beer, the rum, the morning juices and the bottle of habanero sauce on every table.

Belikin

Belikin is the national beer, brewed in Belize and served at almost every restaurant, beach bar and corner shop. The everyday lager is the classic; you may also see a stout and seasonal styles. It pairs naturally with fried fish, ceviche and rice and beans.

Rum

Belize distills its own rum from local sugarcane, and rum punch, rum and coconut water, and panty rippers (coconut rum with pineapple juice) are beach-bar standards. A bottle of local rum and a rum cake are popular souvenirs.

Juices and shakes

Fresh juices are blended to order: watermelon, lime, orange, pineapple, soursop and seasonal mango. The curious can try a seaweed shake, a creamy blend of dried seaweed, milk, cinnamon and nutmeg sold at markets and some cafés.

Coffee

Most coffee drunk in Belize is imported, but small roasters on Ambergris Caye and in Cayo roast island blends, and cafés in the tourist towns serve espresso drinks.

Marie Sharp’s and habanero sauce

Marie Sharp’s is Belize’s best-known hot sauce brand, made in Belize from habanero peppers blended with carrots, onions and other vegetables or fruit. A bottle sits on almost every restaurant table, in heat levels from mild to very hot, and it is one of the most popular food souvenirs. The company’s factory near Dangriga has offered visits.

Hot sauce heat guide

Belizean tables often carry more than one bottle. Carrot-based habanero sauces are fruity and medium; the "hot" and "beware" styles are serious. Try a drop on rice and beans first, then build up. If you overdo it, rice, tortillas and a sip of something sweet calm the burn faster than water.

Non-alcoholic choices

Besides juices and shakes, look for horchata (rice drink) at Mestizo stalls, lime juice sweetened with sugar, coconut water straight from the nut, and local soft drinks. Iced tea and coffee drinks are easy to find in the tourist towns.

Water

Many visitors stick to bottled or filtered water; restaurants in tourist areas generally serve purified water and ice.
